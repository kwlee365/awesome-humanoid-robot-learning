## Definition and scope

This area covers the physical humanoid: actuators and transmissions, limb topology (serial, parallel, tendon-routed), materials and manufacturing, hands, wrists and heads, and the integrated platforms that package all of it for research use. It also covers co-design, where morphology and controller are optimised together. Control algorithms belong to the locomotion and manipulation areas, but the boundary is porous: [Control of Humanoid Robots with Parallel Mechanisms](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/control-of-humanoid-robots-with-parallel-mechanisms-using-kinematic-actuation-mo) exists precisely because mechanism and policy can no longer be specified independently. Several list entries are repositories or project pages rather than papers — [Aero Hand Open](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/aero-hand-open), [Micro-Wheeled_leg-Robot](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/micro-wheeled-leg-robot), [Zeroth Bot](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/zeroth-bot) — and are treated as artefacts of the open-hardware trend, not as evidence for technical claims.

## Why this problem matters

Hardware fixes the feasible set for everything above it. Torque density decides whether a joint can hold a squat; backdrivability decides whether it can be force-controlled without an external sensor; the mechanical shock path decides whether a fall costs a gearbox. Learning-based control sharpens these constraints in two ways. Reinforcement learning explores by executing bad actions, so a platform must survive falls and saturated-torque commands a hand-designed controller would never issue. And sim-to-real transfer is a property of the mechanism as much as of the algorithm: a robot close to a rigid serial chain with identifiable actuators is cheap to simulate faithfully, while springs, cable friction and closed kinematic loops each add modelling error paid for later in randomisation or system identification. Cost and openness matter separately: they set how many groups can run an experiment, and therefore whether a result can be checked.

## Historical development

The first generation of walking humanoids was built to make one thing true: that the joints follow the plan. Honda's P-series (Hirai et al., ICRA 1998; [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/677288)) and the Japanese HRP line — [HRP-2](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1307969) (ICRA 2004), later [HRP-5P](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8630006) (RA-L 2019) (all historical references, not in the list) — used electric motors behind high-ratio harmonic drives with local position servos, six-axis force/torque sensors at ankles and wrists, and a rigid machined structure. This was coherent engineering, not conservatism: the ZMP pipeline of the era assumed a rigid robot tracking a precomputed centre-of-mass trajectory, and high gear ratios plus stiff structure are what make that assumption approximately true. The costs were structural. A harmonic drive at 100:1 is effectively non-backdrivable, so contact forces cannot be inferred from motor current and must be measured by dedicated sensors and regulated by a limited-bandwidth admittance loop. Impacts pass through the gear teeth, so falls are expensive. These machines were also single-copy, closed and priced out of most laboratories; the open exception, iCub (Neural Networks 2010; [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0893608010001619)) (historical reference, not in the list), was notable partly because openness was itself unusual.

The compliance movement attacked force control mechanically. Pratt and Williamson's "Series elastic actuators" (IROS 1995; [IEEE Xplore](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/525827)) (historical reference, not in the list) placed a deliberately soft element between gearbox and load, converting force control into position control of a spring deflection, decoupling the load from reflected motor inertia and giving impact energy somewhere to go; "Series Elastic Actuators for legged robots" (SPIE 2004; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1117/12.548000)) carried the idea into legs, and ATRIAS (IJRR 2016; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364916648388)) built a tether-free 3D biped around leaf springs as an explicit spring-mass template (both historical references, not in the list). The trade was real: the spring imposes a bandwidth ceiling, adds mass and a state to estimate, and makes the plant harder to simulate. Variable-impedance designs such as the DLR Hand Arm System (IJRR 2012; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0278364912459209)) (historical reference, not in the list) pushed the idea furthest and showed how much complexity it costs.

The proprioceptive quasi-direct-drive line took the opposite route: make the transmission transparent instead of adding elasticity. [Proprioceptive actuator design in the MIT Cheetah](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/proprioceptive-actuator-design-in-the-mit-cheetah-impact-mitigation-and-high-ban) argued that a large-gap-radius motor behind a single low-ratio stage gives high torque density with low reflected inertia, so joint torque can be commanded and estimated from motor current, and impacts are mitigated by low inertia rather than by a spring. My reading is that this was the most consequential mechanical idea of the period, because it removed the dependence on load cells and on springs at once. [Mini Cheetah](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1109/ICRA.2019.8793865) (ICRA 2019) (historical reference, not in the list) showed the architecture with commodity brushless motors and planetary gears; [Quasi-Direct Drive for Low-Cost Compliant Robotic Manipulation](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/quasi-direct-drive-for-low-cost-compliant-robotic-manipulation) carried it to a 7-DoF arm reported under 5,000 USD with a stated 7.5 Hz position-control bandwidth; [The MIT Humanoid Robot](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/the-mit-humanoid-robot-design-motion-planning-and-control-for-acrobatic-behavior) applied it to a bipedal body aimed at acrobatic motion. The public record also shows Boston Dynamics retiring hydraulic Atlas for an electric platform in 2024 ([Boston Dynamics blog](https://bostondynamics.com/blog/electric-new-era-for-atlas/)) (historical reference, not in the list).

What turned this from an architecture into a movement was supply chain. Drone and e-bike motors, cheap field-oriented-control drivers and desktop 3D printing made a torque-controllable joint something a graduate student could buy and assemble, and the [Open Dynamic Robot Initiative](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00093) (RA-L 2020) (historical reference, not in the list) published a modular torque-controlled actuator as open hardware. The list records the humanoid consequence: [Berkeley Humanoid](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/berkeley-humanoid-a-research-platform-for-learning-based-control) is designed for low simulation complexity and reliability against falls; [ToddlerBot](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/toddlerbot-open-source-ml-compatible-humanoid-platform-for-loco-manipulation) is entirely 3D-printed under a stated 6,000 USD and reports independent reproduction; [Berkeley Humanoid Lite](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/berkeley-humanoid-lite-an-open-source-accessible-and-customizable-3d-printed-hum) prints even the cycloidal gearboxes and reports under 5,000 USD with zero-shot RL transfer; [AGILOped](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/agiloped-agile-open-source-humanoid-robot-for-research) and [the Duke Humanoid](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/the-duke-humanoid-design-and-control-for-energy-efficient-bipedal-locomotion-usi) continue the line, the latter exploiting passive dynamics for efficiency. Evaluation practice changed with the hardware: bills of materials, assembly documentation, third-party replication and zero-shot sim-to-real transfer became expected reporting, in place of a demonstration video.

Hands and wrists never fitted this story cleanly and became a sub-field with its own trade curve. The tendon tradition runs from the [Utah/M.I.T. Dextrous Hand](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1087395) (1986) through the DLR hand, surveyed in "A Century of Robotic Hands" (Annual Review of Control, Robotics, and Autonomous Systems 2019; [publisher page](https://www.annualreviews.org/content/journals/10.1146/annurev-control-060117-105003)) (historical references, not in the list); the linkage tradition is exemplified by [the ILDA hand](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/integrated-linkage-driven-dexterous-anthropomorphic-robotic-hand), reporting 15 DoF over 20 joints, 34 N fingertip force and 1.1 kg with actuation and electronics inside the palm. Tendons buy compactness and compliance at the price of friction, hysteresis and calibration drift; linkages buy stiffness and repeatability at the price of volume and design effort. Learning altered this trade rather than resolving it: [RUKA](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/ruka-rethinking-the-design-of-humanoid-hands-with-learning) keeps a cheap 3D-printed tendon hand with 15 underactuated DoF and learns the joint-to-actuator and fingertip-to-actuator maps from motion-capture data, turning the nonlinearity that made tendon hands hard to control into a regression problem. Around it sit [ORCA](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/orca-open-source-reliable-cost-effective-anthropomorphic-robotic-hand-for-uninte), which makes uninterrupted operation a design goal, [RAPID Hand](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/rapid-hand-a-robust-affordable-perception-integrated-dexterous-manipulation-plat) with integrated perception, the 20-DoF [ByteDexter](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/dexterous-teleoperation-of-20-dof-bytedexter-hand-via-human-motion-retargeting), the SMA-hybrid [CYJ Hand-0](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/a-21-dof-humanoid-dexterous-hand-with-hybrid-sma-motor-actuation-cyj-hand-0), and a [Bowden-cable antagonistic hand](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/antagonistic-bowden-cable-actuation-of-a-lightweight-robotic-hand-toward-dextero) that moves actuator mass off the payload-constrained forearm. Wrists became a separate design object once dynamic and confined-space manipulation were demanded: [DexWrist](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/dexwrist-a-robotic-wrist-for-constrained-and-dynamic-manipulation) uses a 2-DoF parallel mechanism with quasi-direct-drive actuators, and [ByteWrist](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/bytewrist-a-parallel-robotic-wrist-enabling-flexible-and-anthropomorphic-motion-) nests three linkage stages for compact 3-DoF roll-pitch-yaw. Tactile sensing followed the same open-hardware pattern in [OSMO](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/osmo-open-source-tactile-glove-for-human-to-robot-skill-transfer).

The most recent shift is that the requirements of learning now propagate backwards into the mechanism. Simulation fidelity became a design constraint, which is why parallel actuation needed the explicit treatment of [kinematic actuation models](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/control-of-humanoid-robots-with-parallel-mechanisms-using-kinematic-actuation-mo) and why ToddlerBot foregrounds transferable motor system identification for its digital twin. Durability under exploration became a stated objective rather than an afterthought. And morphology itself became an optimisation variable, in [optimal ankle design](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/a-framework-for-optimal-ankle-design-of-humanoid-robots), the [variable reduction ratio knee](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/explosive-output-to-enhance-jumping-ability-a-variable-reduction-ratio-design-pa), the decoupled-actuation [DecARt Leg](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/decart-leg-design-and-evaluation-of-a-novel-humanoid-robot-leg-with-decoupled-ac) with its Fastest Achievable Swing Time metric, and the position paper [Embracing Evolution](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/embracing-evolution-a-call-for-body-control-co-design-in-embodied-humanoid-robot). My reading is that this exposes a gap: the field has no shared way to say whether one body is better than another, which is what [Human-Level Actuation for Humanoids](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/human-level-actuation-for-humanoids) proposes to fix with measurement protocols rather than datasheet numbers.

## Major technical transitions

Design objectives moved from trajectory-tracking accuracy to force controllability and impact survival, then to trainability — how cheaply the machine simulates and how long it lasts under exploration. Actuation moved from high-ratio harmonic drives, through series elastic and variable-impedance elements, to low-ratio proprioceptive drives, with hydraulics leaving the public humanoid record. The force-sensing interface moved from six-axis load cells to spring deflection to motor current. Manufacturing moved from machined single copies to 3D printing and commodity parts, cost claims from unstated to explicit, and openness from closed platforms to released CAD, firmware and training stacks. Kinematic topology diversified: serial chains gained parallel ankles, wrists and knees, alongside musculoskeletal structures such as those in [PIMBS](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/pimbs-efficient-body-schema-learning-for-musculoskeletal-humanoids). And the design process moved from sequential — build, then control — to joint optimisation.

## Current research frontier

Four threads are active. Co-design is the most visible: joint optimisation of morphology and policy appears for [fall recovery](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/toward-humanoid-brain-body-co-design-joint-optimization-of-control-and-morpholog), [chin-ups](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/evolutionary-continuous-adaptive-rl-powered-co-design-for-humanoid-chin-up-perfo) and [jet-powered flight](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/cad-driven-co-design-for-flight-ready-jet-powered-humanoids), with [iRonCub 3](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/ironcub-3-the-jet-powered-flying-humanoid-robot) the extreme case of a morphology defined by a non-locomotion objective. Second, joint-level mechanism design is being reopened with metrics attached, in [ankle design](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/a-framework-for-optimal-ankle-design-of-humanoid-robots), [variable-reduction-ratio knees](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/explosive-output-to-enhance-jumping-ability-a-variable-reduction-ratio-design-pa) and [DecARt Leg](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/decart-leg-design-and-evaluation-of-a-novel-humanoid-robot-leg-with-decoupled-ac). Third, musculoskeletal humanoids continue as a parallel programme in which redundancy and nonlinearity are absorbed by learned body schemas — [PIMBS](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/pimbs-efficient-body-schema-learning-for-musculoskeletal-humanoids), [exceeding joint speed limits in redundant tendon structures](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/exceeding-the-maximum-speed-limit-of-the-joint-angle-for-the-redundant-tendon-dr), and a 2026 review of [muscle characteristics and management](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/characteristics-management-and-utilization-of-muscles-in-musculoskeletal-humanoi). Fourth, bodies are diversifying away from the generic adult-sized humanoid: [Olaf](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/olaf-bringing-an-animated-character-to-life-in-the-physical-world) reports spherical and planar linkages fitting actuators inside character proportions and feeds actuator temperature into the policy, [Fauna Sprout](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/fauna-sprout-a-lightweight-approachable-developer-ready-humanoid-robot) is presented as a lightweight platform with limited joint torques and soft exteriors for shared spaces, and [DIJIT](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/dijit-a-robotic-head-for-an-active-observer) treats the head as an active-perception mechanism.

## Open problems and unresolved limitations

There is still no accepted way to compare two humanoid bodies. Peak torque, mass and DoF counts are reported inconsistently, and the proposal in [Human-Level Actuation for Humanoids](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/human-level-actuation-for-humanoids) is at present a framework rather than a body of comparative measurements. Durability is the least documented property: papers rarely report cycles to failure, thermal duty limits or mean time between repairs, even though these dominate the practical cost of RL experimentation — Olaf's need to expose actuator temperature to the policy is a rare public acknowledgement that thermal limits bind. Cost figures usually cover a bill of materials only, excluding labour, tooling, calibration and spares, so my reading is that reported cost gaps overstate the difference in total cost of ownership. Hands remain the weakest link: tendon systems drift and wear, linkage hands are bulky, tactile sensing is not standardised despite open efforts such as [OSMO](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/osmo-open-source-tactile-glove-for-human-to-robot-skill-transfer), and no consensus exists on how many DoF are useful. Parallel and tendon-routed mechanisms remain second-class citizens in simulation, so co-design results validated only in simulation should be read as hypotheses. Openness is uneven too: several platforms release CAD and firmware while the actuators stay proprietary.
