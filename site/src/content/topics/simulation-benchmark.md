## Definition and scope

This area covers the infrastructure everything else runs on: physics **engines** that integrate contact dynamics, **task suites and benchmarks** that define success, **asset and scene datasets** that populate tasks, **data-generation pipelines** that turn a few demonstrations into thousands, and **evaluation protocols** that decide whether a number means anything. It also includes learned generative world models when used *as* simulators. These are separable concerns often bundled into one release: a paper announcing "a simulator" may contribute a contact solver, a scene set, a task list, a dataset or a leaderboard — usually a subset, rarely all.

## Why this problem matters

Humanoid hardware is expensive, slow to reset and easy to break, so nearly all learning happens in simulation and only the final policy touches the robot. The simulator therefore becomes the de facto definition of the problem. If the contact model is wrong, policies learn to exploit it; if the task suite contains only what the engine simulates well, measured progress drifts toward whatever is easy to simulate. Benchmarks compound this — a shared benchmark is what lets two labs compare methods, but one that does not correlate with hardware outcomes will efficiently coordinate the field on the wrong thing.

## Historical development

The first generation of robotics simulators was general-purpose, CPU-bound and built for interactivity, not throughput. [Bullet and ODE](https://github.com/bulletphysics/bullet3) (historical reference, not in the list) came from games and graphics; [Gazebo](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2004.1389727) (2004; historical reference, not in the list) wrapped them for research and became the ROS default. The decisive contribution for learning was [MuJoCo](https://doi.org/10.1109/IROS.2012.6386109) (Todorov et al., IROS 2012; historical reference, not in the list), whose soft-constraint contact formulation in generalised coordinates gave smooth, well-conditioned dynamics suitable for gradient- and sampling-based control — a modelling choice made for *control*, not visual realism. Legged robotics pushed on the solver directly with [RaiSim's per-contact iteration method](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/8255551) (Hwangbo et al., RA-L 2018; historical reference, not in the list), aimed at the hard, many-contact regime legged machines live in.

Contact-rich legged simulation was slow for structural reasons, not merely engineering ones: contact resolution is a coupled complementarity or optimisation problem whose cost grows with simultaneous contact count, it resists the embarrassing parallelism that made rendering fast, and control-rate stability forces small timesteps. Engines also disagreed, as documented empirically by [SimBenchmark](https://leggedrobotics.github.io/SimBenchmark/about/sims.html) (historical reference, not in the list), which compared RaiSim, Bullet, ODE, MuJoCo and DART rather than assuming interchangeability.

A second thread separated from the engine: the **task suite**. [OpenAI Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/1606.01540) (2016) established that an environment is an API plus a reward, and the [DeepMind Control Suite](https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.00690) (2018) fixed standard MuJoCo tasks so algorithms could be compared rather than demonstrated; manipulation followed with [RLBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/1909.12271) and [robosuite](https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.12293). [Habitat](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.01201) and [SAPIEN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.08515) contributed the third thread — **assets and scenes**, a data problem rather than a physics problem (all historical references, not in the list).

The GPU-parallel turn is the clearest discontinuity here. [Isaac Gym](https://arxiv.org/abs/2108.10470) (2021; historical reference, not in the list) kept state, physics and rollout entirely on the GPU, and [Brax](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.13281) (2021; historical reference, not in the list) did the analogous thing in JAX with differentiability. What changed was not only wall-clock time: with thousands of environments stepping together, on-policy RL with enormous batches became practical, wide domain randomisation became nearly free because each randomised instance is one more environment, and reward shaping could be tuned by trial. Most humanoid locomotion work after 2022 presupposes this regime. [Orbit, later Isaac Lab](https://arxiv.org/abs/2301.04195) (2023; historical reference, not in the list) then rebuilt the missing task and asset layer on top.

Consolidation recombined the threads. [ManiSkill3](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/maniskill3-gpu-parallelized-robotics-simulation-and-rendering-for-generalizable-) parallelised rendering as well as physics, which matters because visual policies were bottlenecked on image generation rather than dynamics; its authors report over 30,000 FPS in benchmarked environments and 10-1000x speedups with 2-3x less GPU memory than other platforms — their own measurements, reported here as their claim. [MuJoCo Playground](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/mujoco-playground) (a library release with a technical report, not a conventional paper) brought MuJoCo's contact model into the GPU regime via MJX, notable because MuJoCo was the engine most trusted for contact-heavy legged work, and [Genesis](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/genesis-a-generative-and-universal-physics-engine-for-robotics-and-beyond) (a project-page release rather than a paper) pushed multi-material physics with generative scene creation. The engine layer keeps moving: [ComFree-Sim](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/comfree-sim-a-gpu-parallelized-analytical-contact-physics-engine-for-scalable-co) returns to the solver with closed-form contact impulses from an impedance-style prediction-correction update in the dual cone of Coulomb friction, claiming near-linear scaling in contact count — evidence that parallelism alone did not fix dense contact. [Newton](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/linux-foundation-announces-contribution-of-newton-by-disney-research-google-deepmind-and-nvidia-to-accelerate-open-robot-learning) (2025; historical reference, not in the list) attempts to make the GPU-era engine a shared open substrate.

Humanoid-specific benchmarks arrived only in 2024. [HumanoidBench](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/humanoidbench-simulated-humanoid-benchmark-for-whole-body-locomotion-and-manipul) supplied whole-body locomotion and manipulation with dexterous hands and reported that state-of-the-art RL struggled on most tasks while hierarchical approaches over robust low-level policies did better — designed to be hard rather than solved. [Humanoid-Gym](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/humanoid-gym-reinforcement-learning-for-humanoid-robot-with-zero-shot-sim2real-t) built the sim-to-sim check into the harness, [BiGym](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/bigym-a-demo-driven-mobile-bi-manual-manipulation-benchmark) made demonstrations part of the benchmark definition, [Mimicking-Bench](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/mimicking-bench-a-benchmark-for-generalizable-humanoid-scene-interaction-learnin) targeted humanoid-scene interaction via human mimicking, and [ManiSkill-HAB](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/maniskill-hab-a-benchmark-for-low-level-manipulation-in-home-rearrangement-tasks) re-benchmarked *low-level* manipulation inside home-rearrangement tasks previously evaluated with abstracted actions.

Scene and asset scale grew in parallel. [RoboCasa](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/robocasa-large-scale-simulation-of-everyday-tasks-for-generalist-robots) used text-to-3D and text-to-image generation to populate kitchens across 100 tasks and reported a scaling trend from synthetic data; [GRUtopia](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/grutopia-dream-general-robots-in-a-city-at-scale) went to city scale with 100,000 annotated scenes plus LLM-driven NPCs. The line continues in [RoboCasa365](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/robocasa365-a-large-scale-simulation-framework-for-training-and-benchmarking-gen) (365 tasks, 2,500 kitchens, 600 hours human plus 1,600 hours synthetic demonstrations) and [MolmoSpaces](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/molmospaces-a-large-scale-open-ecosystem-for-robot-navigation-and-manipulation) (over 230,000 indoor environments, 130,000 annotated assets, backed by MuJoCo, Isaac and ManiSkill).

**Data generation** became its own artefact once demonstrations, not compute, were the binding constraint. [MimicGen](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.17596) (2023; historical reference, not in the list) set the pattern: segment a few human demonstrations into object-centric subtasks and replay them under new object poses. [DexMimicGen](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/dexmimicgen-automated-data-generation-for-bimanual-dexterous-manipulation-via-im) extended it to bimanual dexterous humanoid manipulation, where inter-arm coordination makes naive replay invalid, and [HumanoidGen](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/humanoidgen-data-generation-for-bimanual-dexterous-manipulation-via-llm-reasonin) replaced hand-authored task structure with LLM-planned constraint sequences over annotated affordances plus MCTS for long horizons. My reading: this changes the epistemics, because the *task distribution itself* becomes a modelling choice rather than an observation.

The newest thread treats a learned generative model as the simulator. [Humanoid World Models](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/humanoid-world-models-open-world-foundation-models-for-humanoid-robotics) trains masked-transformer and flow-matching models to forecast egocentric video conditioned on humanoid control tokens, sized for one or two GPUs, and the [1X World Model Challenge report](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/generative-world-modelling-for-humanoids-1x-world-model-challenge-technical-repo) shows the idea benchmarked competitively on real humanoid data across sampling and compression tracks. These are scored on prediction quality (PSNR, cross-entropy), not physical consistency — an open substitution.

Underneath everything sits benchmark validity, addressed until recently only indirectly through sim-to-sim gates and cross-engine agreement. [MolmoSpaces](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/molmospaces-a-large-scale-open-ecosystem-for-robot-navigation-and-manipulation) reports sim-real correlation of R = 0.96 and rho = 0.98 on its suite — the kind of evidence the area has needed, though it is one ecosystem's self-report over a chosen task set. [DualTHOR](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/dualthor-a-dual-arm-humanoid-simulation-platform-for-contingency-aware-planning) attacks the gap from the modelling side, arguing that platforms bypassing the stochastic nature of low-level execution overstate transferability. Metric design became a topic in its own right: [Motion Difficulty](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/benchmarking-humanoid-imitation-learning-with-motion-difficulty) argues joint-error metrics conflate policy quality with intrinsic motion difficulty and proposes a dynamics-grounded difficulty score with a stratified repartition of AMASS, while [Towards Motion Turing Test](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/towards-motion-turing-test-evaluating-human-likeness-in-humanoid-robots) makes human judgement the metric and reports humanoid motion still distinguishable from human motion, especially in dynamic actions.

## Major technical transitions

Engines moved from CPU soft-constraint solvers built for control, through per-contact iterative solvers for legged hardware, to GPU-resident batched physics, and now to analytical GPU contact formulations for dense contact. Parallelism moved from many processes on many cores to thousands of environments in one kernel launch, changing the algorithms people use and not just their speed. Assets moved from hand-authored URDFs to scanned scenes to generative asset pipelines. Task definition moved from hand-written rewards to demonstration-defined tasks to LLM-composed task graphs. Data moved from teleoperation to replay-based augmentation to fully synthetic generation. Evaluation moved from single-task reward curves to standardised suites, then sim-to-sim gates, and now toward measured sim-real correlation, difficulty stratification and human perceptual judgement.

## Representative papers

- [HumanoidBench](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/humanoidbench-simulated-humanoid-benchmark-for-whole-body-locomotion-and-manipul) — whole-body suite that standard RL largely fails, with hierarchical baselines doing better.
- [Humanoid-Gym](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/humanoid-gym-reinforcement-learning-for-humanoid-robot-with-zero-shot-sim2real-t) — harness making cross-engine sim-to-sim verification part of the workflow.
- [ManiSkill3](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/maniskill3-gpu-parallelized-robotics-simulation-and-rendering-for-generalizable-) — GPU parallelisation of rendering alongside physics, with the authors' own throughput comparisons.
- [MuJoCo Playground](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/mujoco-playground) — library release bringing MuJoCo/MJX into GPU-accelerated legged sim-to-real workflows.

- [ComFree-Sim](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/comfree-sim-a-gpu-parallelized-analytical-contact-physics-engine-for-scalable-co) — analytical closed-form contact impulses on GPU, targeting near-linear scaling in dense contact.
- [RoboCasa](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/robocasa-large-scale-simulation-of-everyday-tasks-for-generalist-robots) and [RoboCasa365](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/robocasa365-a-large-scale-simulation-framework-for-training-and-benchmarking-gen) — generative-asset kitchens, then a 365-task benchmark with human and synthetic demonstrations.

## Current research frontier

Four things move at once. Contact solvers are being redesigned for GPU execution rather than ported to it, as in [ComFree-Sim](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/comfree-sim-a-gpu-parallelized-analytical-contact-physics-engine-for-scalable-co), with shared substrates like [Newton](https://www.linuxfoundation.org/press/linux-foundation-announces-contribution-of-newton-by-disney-research-google-deepmind-and-nvidia-to-accelerate-open-robot-learning) (historical reference, not in the list) trying to reduce per-lab engine fragmentation. Ecosystems are replacing simulators: [MolmoSpaces](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/molmospaces-a-large-scale-open-ecosystem-for-robot-navigation-and-manipulation) and [RoboCasa365](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/robocasa365-a-large-scale-simulation-framework-for-training-and-benchmarking-gen) bundle assets, tasks, demonstrations and evaluation with swappable backends. Evaluation is being treated as a measurement problem, including cloud-hosted standardised evaluation as in [Humanoid Everyday](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/humanoid-everyday-a-comprehensive-robotic-dataset-for-open-world-humanoid-manipu). And learned world models are being positioned as complements to physics engines, though the two are judged on incomparable criteria. Low-barrier teaching infrastructure occupies its own niche, as in [pyCub](/awesome-humanoid-robot-learning/papers/learning-with-pycub-a-simulation-and-exercise-framework-for-humanoid-robotics).

## Open problems and unresolved limitations

Benchmark validity is the central unresolved issue: one ecosystem reporting high sim-real correlation on its own tasks is progress, but there is no accepted procedure for establishing that a benchmark predicts hardware behaviour, and few published negative results. Contact fidelity is second — no engine claims accurate modelling of deformable pads, hand-object friction, impact and backlash at humanoid scale, and the cross-engine disagreement documented in [SimBenchmark](https://leggedrobotics.github.io/SimBenchmark/about/sims.html) has been routed around via sim-to-sim checks and randomisation rather than resolved. Third, generated data plus generated tasks risk a closed loop in which policies are trained on synthetic distributions and evaluated on suites from the same generative process, measuring self-consistency rather than capability. Fourth, learned world models carry no notion of physical validity, so success inside one says little about surviving contact. Fifth, throughput reporting has no shared standard: speed figures come from authors' own configurations, making cross-paper comparison unreliable. Finally, humanoid benchmarks remain fragmented by embodiment, and no cross-embodiment evaluation protocol exists.
